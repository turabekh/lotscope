### 2.1 Screening and valuation (ADR-0072, ADR-0073)

A production parcel read **"Strong candidate — margin 55% and no elevated risk signal"** directly above three risk flags and "No comps". Both halves were produced correctly by the rules and both read wrongly. The margin rested on the assessed value because no AVM estimate existed, and `CRITICAL_FIELDS` covered only buildability so valuation availability never reached the decision. "No elevated risk signal" was true against a threshold of 60 at a score of 30.

**4,421 parcels had been presenting as strong candidates on a margin priced from an assessment.**

ADR-0073 then asked whether those parcels truly have no comps or whether the window was too tight. Measured on 400 of the actually-downgraded parcels: 398 had zero comps, and **338 were recoverable** by widening to 3,000 ft / 5 years with residential↔commercial interchange.

Two measurements decided the design and are worth keeping:

- **Allowing improved-land sales recovers all 398 and is invalid** — such a sale prices the structure, producing a confident-looking number that measures the wrong thing.
- **Restricting cross-use to residential↔commercial costs nothing** — permitting all four classes also recovers exactly 338, so industrial and special stay segregated rather than being allowed for free.

The widened search runs **only when the tight query returns nothing**, so the 94% of parcels with good local comps cannot be diluted. A wide-area estimate is forced to `low` confidence (the confidence formula reads only count and dispersion — distance is not in it) and **never earns `strong_candidate`**.

### 2.2 The near-miss worth remembering

`recompute_vacant_verdicts` runs nightly at 07:00 UTC **with no market filter**. An ungated fallback would have reached every market the night it deployed. Proportionally the pilots are far more exposed — 18.5% of Philadelphia verdicts carry no valuation, against **91.9% for Pittsburgh and 100% for Harrisburg** — and 3,000 ft spans a large share of a city the size of Harrisburg.

The fallback is therefore gated to markets where its window was measured, via a registry in `valuation/fallback.py`. Philadelphia is the only entry. Extending it needs the same 400-parcel measurement first — that is a decision about pilot quality, not a config change.

## 3. The recompute, and what it actually did

101,868 Philadelphia verdicts had no stored valuation. All were recomputed — verified complete: the hourly update buckets sum to exactly 101,868, and the still-unvalued rows are spread evenly across the parcel-number range rather than clustered at the end.

| recommendation | before | after | change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `strong_candidate` | 10,053 | **10,053** | **0** |
| `worth_investigating` | 10,284 | 11,674 | **+1,390** |
| `economics_unattractive` | 188,664 | 187,274 | −1,390 |
| `high_entitlement_risk` · `insufficient_data` · `likely_pass` | — | — | unchanged |

**Rows carrying a land value: 101,868 unvalued → 25,753. 76,115 parcels gained one.**

**`strong_candidate` moved by zero rows.** That is ADR-0073's central invariant confirmed in production rather than only in tests: a wide-area estimate yields a real value and margin but can never promote a parcel. The 1,390 that moved were lots that looked unviable only because the land could not be priced.

Verified live afterwards on `parcelphl.online`: *"No land sales were found close by, so this uses sales up to 3,000 ft away and up to 5 years old — a rough guide, not a market read for this block."*

**The recovery rate was 74.7%, not the 85% predicted.** The 85% came from sampling the 4,421 strong-margin downgrades and was then applied to all 101,868 unvalued rows — a population that also includes `insufficient_data` and non-vacant parcels with different comp availability. Extrapolating from that subset was unjustified. **When sizing a population, sample the population you are going to act on.**

**LR-B-016 is the sharpest of those.** `_confidence()` reads two inputs — is lot area known, is lot width known. LR-B004 requires seven. ## 5. Process — what went wrong and what caught things

**I branched off a feature branch instead of `main` twice.** The first time silently pulled the handoff commit into #103, leaving #102 redundant (closed with an explanation). The second time I caught it and rebased. Both were the same lapse: not checking the current branch before `git checkout -b`. **After any `gh pr create` or `gh pr merge`, run `git checkout main && git pull` before starting the next branch.**

**`make check` could not catch a formatting failure CI enforces.** It ran `ruff check` and `pnpm lint` but neither format check. **`make fmt-check` now exists and `check` depends on it** — and it caught a real prettier failure within the hour.

**A green e2e suite proved nothing about the Risk tab.** No spec had ever opened it, and every seeded parcel has a unique owner, so all six would have shown the blank. Two specs now open it.

**An e2e failure that was not mine.** #104 failed during SSL negotiation to Postgres, before any of my code ran. I re-ran rather than asserting "flaky", and it passed unchanged.

**A false liveness signal.** I reported the recompute process as possibly dead because `ps` returned 0 — `ps` is not installed in the worker container. Reading `/proc` directly showed it alive. **Check that your instrument exists before believing its answer.**

**The background job outlived its own SSH.** When the local background command was killed, the remote `docker compose exec` kept running but the completion notification was lost. A replacement watcher was started. If a long remote job matters, watch the *remote* process, not the local wrapper.

**I estimated this recompute wrong three times** — low from batch count, high from a partial sample, then 85% from a biased subset. The measured rate on the machine that will run it is the only number worth quoting.
